Section 1
Program Philosophy
This isn't just a workout plan — it's a set of beliefs about what we're doing and why. Read this section before anything else. If you understand the philosophy, the rest of the packet will make sense.
Program Identity
What We're Building

We train teenagers to become solid young adults. The goal is never to manufacture superstars or chase individual glory — it's to build a program of disciplined, coachable athletes who compete with effort, professionalism, and class.

We train for team championships. That mindset shapes everything from how we practice to how we show up at meets. Personal records matter, but they are a byproduct of doing things right — not the end goal in themselves.

Dignity and Class
The Daily Standard

This isn't just a meet-day expectation — it's how we operate every single day. Athletes carry themselves with professionalism in practice, compete with respect for their competitors, and represent Ramona with class in every setting.

How we treat other teams, officials, and the sport itself is as important as what we run. We do not have divas on this team. We do not talk trash. We do not cut corners. We win and lose with dignity.

Training Philosophy
Modern Sprint Development — Quality Over Volume

We are in the modern school of sprint development. Training is purposeful, intense, and deliberate — not high-mileage, grind-it-out work. Every rep has a reason. Every session has an intent. Athletes run fast, rest fully, and focus completely.

Senseless volume is not the goal. We do not run 100s to train for the 100. We do not run until athletes are broken. We isolate the phase of the sprint we are developing — acceleration, max velocity, or endurance — and we train that phase with quality. This program is Altis-based. Altis (altis.world) is a world-class track and field performance center whose methods form the foundation of how we structure training. Study their work.

Section 2
Coaching Rules
These are Coach Lewis's personal rules for running this program, combined with the foundational principles from The Sprint Project's "5 Golden Rules." These are not suggestions — they are what separates a good practice from a wasted one.
Coach Lewis's Rules
1
Every Rep Has a Purpose
If you can't tell the athlete why they're doing a rep, don't assign it. Every workout, every drill, every set should have an intent. Athletes who understand the purpose of what they're doing buy in harder and execute better.
2
Train Gradual, Phase By Phase
Do not jump to Speed work before Endurance and Strength phases are complete. Build the foundation first. Shortcuts in training show up as injuries and plateaus during the season.
3
Rest Is Not Optional — It's Part of the Workout
In Speed and Strength phases, rest intervals are prescribed for a reason. Cutting rest short means the next rep is lower quality. If the goal is high-speed, high-intensity output, the athlete must be recovered to produce it. Don't skip rest.
4
Culture Is Non-Negotiable
We run with dignity and class. This is enforced daily, not just on meet days. An athlete who disrespects another team, a coach, or the sport is an athlete who is embarrassing everything we stand for. Address it immediately.
5
Know What You Don't Know
This packet is a starting point, not an end point. Coach Lewis's knowledge is not the final word on sprint coaching — it's a foundation. When you're unsure, reference the Altis resources and The Sprint Project materials included here. Keep learning.
The 5 Golden Rules (The Sprint Project)
These are universal sprint training principles backed by research. Apply them to every phase of the season.
1
48–72 Hour Recovery Between Hard Sessions
After a high-intensity sprint session, the National Strength and Conditioning Association recommends 48–72 hours before another hard session. The nervous system takes longer to recover than the muscles. Respect this or athletes will plateau and break down.
2
Quality Over Quantity
Sprinting more does not make you faster if the quality is low. High-quality sprints require being well rested, using max or near-max effort, and executing proper technique. A tired athlete running sloppy reps is building bad habits, not speed.
3
1 Step Back, 2 Steps Forward
If an athlete is exhausted or close to injury, it's better to take one step back and recover so they can take two steps forward in long-term progress. Pain warns gradually. Listen to it before it becomes an injury that costs weeks.
4
If You Can't Measure It, You Can't Improve It
Track athlete times — 30m acceleration, 30m fly, 10m fly are the key sprint metrics. Without tracking, you're guessing. With tracking, you can see what's working and what's not. Keep records.
5
Sleep Is the Magic Pill
Sleep is when the muscles, CNS, and brain recover and recharge. Athletes sleeping less than 7–9 hours are limiting their own performance. Remind your athletes that recovery happens off the track too.

Technique & Diagnosis
Your job as a sprint coach is to watch athletes run and know what you're looking at. This section gives you the tools to identify technique problems and prescribe the right corrective exercises. Start with the checklists, then use the flowchart to match mistakes to solutions.
How To Use This System

Step 1: Watch the athlete run. Use the three checklists (Start, Horizontal, Vertical) to score their technique. You're looking for what's missing.

Step 2: Identify which category their weakness falls into — Start, Horizontal (forward drive), or Vertical (leg cycle).

Step 3: Match the mistake to the Solutions Flowchart below. Select 2–3 corrective exercises and add them to the technical section of their next session.

Step 4: Stay on those exercises until you see improvement, then reassess. Don't keep changing what you're working on.

Relays
Relay System
The relay is one of the most important events for team scoring. Exchange execution wins and loses relay races — not just individual speed. Every sprinter on this team should know the relay system cold.
The Most Important Rule
CURVE = INSIDE = RIGHT HAND
STRAIGHT = OUTSIDE = LEFT HAND
Leg Assignments
🔵
1st Leg (Straight Start)
Starts on the straight. Carries the baton on the left hand (outside). Usually your best starter — not necessarily your fastest athlete. The 1st leg sets up everything.
🔴
2nd Leg (Curve Exchange)
Receives on the straight and runs the curve. Receives left, carries right. Often your most technical athlete — must handle a curve and a blind pass.
🔵
3rd Leg (Straight Exchange)
Receives on the curve and runs the straight. Receives right, carries left. A strong straight runner. This exchange is often where races are won or lost on timing.
⭐
Anchor (4th Leg — Curve Finish)
Receives on the straight and runs the curve to the finish. Your fastest and most mentally tough athlete. The anchor carries the team home.
Practice Progression
Weekly Relay Practice Structure

Week 1–2 (Intro): General info on relay rules and strategy. Groups of 4 running 2 laps together. Walk-through handoffs. Zone jog-throughs × 6.

Weeks 3–5 (Development): Zone work at 50% / 75% / 90%. Focus on go mark timing and hand-off mechanics at each intensity level.

Weeks 6+ (Race Prep): Near-race-speed exchanges. Optional blocks. Rehearse full relay sequences. Identify A/B/JV teams.

Section 8
Recovery & ELDOA
Recovery is not optional — it's training. Athletes who recover well perform better. ELDOA is a recovery and mobility system used in this program on recovery days, after hard workouts, and as part of the active recovery rotation.
What is ELDOA?

ELDOA (Étirements Longitudinaux avec Decoaptation Ostéo-Articulaire) is a therapeutic movement system designed to create space in the spine and joints. Each posture targets a specific spinal segment through myofascial tension. For sprinters, ELDOA addresses lower back compression, hip flexor tightness, and joint decompression that accumulates from high-speed training.

Positions are held for 1–2 minutes each. Athletes should feel the stretch deepen progressively — it is not a passive stretch but an active, sustained hold.

The 6 ELDOA Positions
Steeple
Seated cross-legged with arms overhead, hands interlaced. Targets upper thoracic spine and shoulder mobility. Common in warm-down and recovery sessions.
Small Globe
Seated with feet together, knees out, arms raised overhead. Targets L5/S1 junction and hip flexors. One of the most commonly used positions in the program.
Large Globe
Seated in a wide straddle with arms raised overhead. Targets thoracolumbar fascia and hip adductors. Good for post-sled and post-block work.
Hurdle Turn
Kneeling lunge with trail leg back, one arm reaching overhead. Named for the hurdling position it mimics. Targets hip flexor, rotator, and lumbar spine.
Front Inline
Kneeling with hips squared and one arm reaching forward-up while the other stays back. Targets thoracic rotation and hip extension. Key for sprinters with rotation deficits.
Todler
Supine (on back) with legs up the wall, toes dorsiflexed, arms out to the sides. Deep lower back decompression. Best used at the end of hard training days.

How To Program

The Season Arc
How the Season Builds

Pre-Season (Weeks 1–4) = Endurance Phase. Volume is king. Broken ladders, hill runs, 4 corners, bodybuilding lifts. The body is being prepared to handle training. Athletes need base conditioning before you push speed.

Season Weeks 1–3 = Transition to Strength. Volume begins to drop. Sleds, plyometrics, power lifting, relay intro. Pace targets tighten. Block work begins.

Season Weeks 4+ = Strength into Speed. 200 repeats, lactic training, zone work at race pace. Lift focus shifts to explosiveness. Start quality becomes the priority. Athletes should feel fast.

Post-Season = Specialization. After championships, the program shifts to individualized speed work and specialized sessions for athletes continuing into the off-season. Lower volume, higher intent. This is where you develop next year's varsity athletes.

Building a Week
Weekly Template Logic

The standard week follows this pattern — you don't need to copy it exactly, but understand why it's structured this way:

Monday: Speed or Explosive. Fresh legs. High output. Full rest between reps. This is the most important practice day of the week.

Tuesday: Conditioning. Volume work. Broken ladders, 200 repeats, lactic. Lower intensity than Monday but physically demanding.

Wednesday: Off, Recovery, or Meet Prep. Allow CNS recovery before Thursday competition.

Thursday: Event Practice, Meet, or Technical Work. Specificity day.

Friday: Off, Recovery, or Light Prep.

Saturday: Hills (pre-season) or Invitational Meet (season).

Common Programming Mistakes to Avoid

From "Smashing PRs 101" (Sprint Project) — 10 reasons athletes plateau:

✗
No Periodization
Training without planned phases. This program is built on periodization — if you abandon the phase structure, athletes will plateau.
✗
Training the 100 by Running 100s
We do not run full 100s to improve at the 100. We target specific zones — 0-30m, 30-60m, 60m+ — with dedicated drills and pacing.
✗
Skipping the Weight Room
Lifting directly improves sprint force output. It also prevents injury. The weight room is not optional for this program.
✗
Too Much Volume
More is not better. 2–3 hard sprint sessions per week maximum. Quality degrades fast with overtraining and injury risk spikes.
✗
Same Thing Every Week
Athletes need new stimuli to keep adapting. Rotate workout variables (distance, rest, intensity) across weeks. Use the ladder progression as the model.
✗
Tracking Zero Metrics
Keep time records. Track 30m acceleration and 30m fly splits. Without data, you can't tell what's working. Use meet results AND practice timing.
